What rendering them showed

  • Arrows are faint, shapes are strong. At code size, thin arrows such as ⇥ and ≔ nearly vanish. Squares, circles, diamonds and the logic signs carry well. The first draft used ⇥ for exit and ≔ for binding; both were replaced after the first render.
  • Mathematical angle brackets read as parentheses, so generics keep < >.
  • Font coverage is the real cost. Of the fonts on this Mac, DejaVu Sans Mono covers every glyph used here and Menlo lacks only ⊢ and ⊥. SF Mono, Cascadia Code, Monaco and Courier New miss most of the arrows and logic signs. This page embeds a DejaVu subset. A glyph syntax needs a blessed font, as Julia, APL and BQN have.
  • Some characters turn into emoji. ▶ ↩ ▪ ↔ and others have emoji variants that certain platforms draw in color. None are used here.
  • Stored rails are fragile. Box characters only join at one exact line height, which is why the Rails panels are set tighter than the rest. They also re-prefix every line when nesting changes. An editor can draw the same rails from plain indentation.
  • Words remain the input. Every glyph has a word spelling. A person or an agent can type exit; the formatter stores ■.

What I would pick

Flow's layout with Notation's restraint: about twenty glyphs for flow, logic, proof and ownership; words for declarations and rare operations; indentation for blocks; and rails drawn by the editor from that indentation, not stored in the file.

Glyphs earn their place where the reader recognizes structure by shape and position. Names and rare concepts must be recalled, and for those a word is faster than a symbol seen twice a month.
